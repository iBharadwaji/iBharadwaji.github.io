Every Ethernet frame starts with the same bit pattern.
Seven bytes of alternating 1s and 0s, then 10101011. That last byte, the Start Frame Delimiter, is how the receiver knows where your data begins.

The circuit that spots it is a sequence detector: one of the first things you learn in digital design, and one of the most common RTL interview questions.

The same circuit runs all over real silicon:
→ A CAN bus controller flags six identical bits in a row as a stuff error.
→ SerDes links such as PCIe Gen1/2 and SATA hunt for the K28.5 comma to find where each 10-bit symbol starts.
→ Every USB 2.0 packet opens with a SYNC field that the receiver locks onto.

It sounds simple. Then the questions start:
• The stream 101011 contains 1011. Does your FSM fire? Many first designs don't.
• How many matches are in 1011011, one or two? Both answers can be right.
• Mealy or Moore: why does one cycle of latency change your timing budget?
• Swap the FSM for a shift register: why can it fire right after reset?
• Make the pattern 64 bits wide at GHz rates. What breaks first?

Interviewers frame this question at least 24 ways. One rule builds every version: the state is the longest prefix of the pattern that the incoming bits end with.
